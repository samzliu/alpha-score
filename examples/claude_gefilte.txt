# In Defense of Gefilte Fish

Some foods get mocked so often that the joke replaces the food. Gefilte fish is one of them. Say the words and most people picture a gray, wobbly oval in cloudy jelly, sold in a glass jar on the bottom shelf of the supermarket. It's the punchline of every Passover seder and the dish your cousin dares you to eat.

I think it deserves better. Gefilte fish has a long history, it was a clever answer to real problems, and when it's made well it's delicious.

## What It Actually Is

"Gefilte" is Yiddish for "stuffed." The original dish, from medieval Central and Eastern Europe, was a whole fish: you took the flesh out carefully, keeping the skin intact, chopped the flesh with onions, eggs, matzo meal or bread, and seasoning, then packed the mixture back into the skin and poached it. It came to the table looking like a fish again, and you sliced it into rounds.

Over time cooks skipped the skin and shaped the mixture into patties or loaves. Those are the ovals we know today. The name stayed, even though nothing gets stuffed anymore.

## Why It Exists

Gefilte fish caught on among Ashkenazi Jews for practical reasons, and some religious ones too.

**Shabbat rules.** Traditional Jewish law forbids *borer*, separating the bad from the good, on the Sabbath. Picking bones out of fish counts. If the fish is deboned and ground ahead of time, you can eat it on Shabbat without worrying about it.

**Making a little go a long way.** Fish was expensive for poor families in the shtetls of Poland, Lithuania, and Ukraine. Grind it, mix it with filler, and one carp could feed a whole table.

**Keeping without a fridge.** Poached fish set in its own jelly kept well in a cold cellar. You could cook it Friday and serve it through Saturday.

## The Great Sugar Divide

A fault line runs through Ashkenazi cooking, and gefilte fish sits right on top of it. Jews from Poland traditionally made their gefilte fish sweet, sometimes very sweet. Jews from Lithuania (the *Litvaks*) made theirs savory and peppery. Food historians have even drawn a rough "gefilte fish line" across Eastern Europe that tracks this split, and it lines up surprisingly well with other cultural and linguistic borders.

If your family has fought about whether gefilte fish should taste like dessert, you're carrying on a debate that's several centuries old.

## The Jar Problem

Gefilte fish's bad name mostly comes from the 20th century. When Jewish immigrants settled in America, companies started selling it in jars to save home cooks a full day of grinding and poaching. The jarred version was cheap and convenient and kept forever, and it tasted like all three. The jelly, which at home is a by-product of the fish stock, became the main thing people remembered.

A couple of generations grew up knowing only the jar, and the dish's reputation tanked.

## The Comeback

Over the last fifteen years or so, gefilte fish has had a quiet revival. Delis and small producers began making it from scratch with better fish, often whitefish, pike, or salmon instead of carp, and served it without the jelly. Some bake it as a terrine. Some sell it frozen in loaves, ready to slice. Chefs have tried smoked versions, herb-heavy versions, and versions with citrus.

Made fresh and served cold, it's light and delicate, a bit like a French quenelle, which is basically a fancier cousin. A good piece of gefilte fish tastes clean, with sweetness from the onion and carrot and a soft, springy texture.

## How to Eat It

- **With horseradish.** This is required. The red kind, made with beets, is traditional and adds color and sweetness. The white kind is sharper. Either way, the heat is what makes the dish come alive.
- **Cold, with a carrot coin on top.** The classic garnish: a slice of carrot poached in the same broth.
- **As an appetizer.** It's a first course. A slice or two on a lettuce leaf is plenty.
- **With good challah or matzo,** depending on the holiday.

## A Simple Approach at Home

If you want to try making it, you don't need to stuff a whole carp. A basic version goes like this:

1. Grind about 2 pounds of mild white fish (whitefish, pike, cod, or a mix) with 1 large onion and 1 carrot.
2. Mix in 2 eggs, about 1/3 cup of matzo meal, salt, white pepper, and a pinch of sugar (or a big pinch, if your family is Polish).
3. With wet hands, shape the mixture into ovals.
4. Gently simmer them for about an hour in a broth made from fish bones, onion, carrot, salt, and pepper.
5. Let them cool in the broth, then chill.

Serve cold with plenty of horseradish. It takes an afternoon, and the first bite will tell you it was worth it.

## Give It Another Chance

Gefilte fish is a dish of resourcefulness. It came from poverty, from religious law, and from cooks who turned one cheap fish into a holiday meal. The jar did it no favors, but the real thing is still around and easier to find than it's been in decades.

So this year, when the platter comes around, skip the joke and take a piece. Add more horseradish than you think you need. You might like it.
